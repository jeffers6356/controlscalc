How Frequency and RPM Are Related

The rotational speed of an AC motor is closely related to the frequency of the electrical power supplied to it. Motor speed also depends on the number of magnetic poles in the motor.

This relationship is especially useful when working with motors, variable-frequency drives (VFDs), tachometers, speed feedback, and process equipment driven by AC motors.

Frequency → RPM Formula

The theoretical speed of the rotating magnetic field is called synchronous speed.

Synchronous RPM = (120 × Frequency) ÷ Number of Poles

Where:

  • Frequency = electrical frequency in Hz
  • Number of Poles = motor magnetic pole count
  • RPM = synchronous speed in revolutions per minute

The factor 120 converts electrical frequency into mechanical revolutions per minute while accounting for the motor's number of poles.

RPM → Frequency Formula

If you know the desired synchronous speed and motor pole count, the formula can be rearranged to calculate frequency.

Frequency = (RPM × Number of Poles) ÷ 120

This is particularly useful when determining the theoretical VFD frequency required to produce a particular motor speed.

Worked Example: 60 Hz, 4-Pole Motor

Suppose a 4-pole motor is supplied with 60 Hz power.

RPM = (120 × 60) ÷ 4
RPM = 1800

Therefore, the motor's theoretical synchronous speed is:

60 Hz = 1800 RPM synchronous speed

An actual induction motor will normally run somewhat below this speed because of motor slip. A typical nameplate speed for a 4-pole motor may therefore be around 1750 RPM rather than exactly 1800 RPM.

Why Motor Poles Matter

Motor poles determine the speed of the rotating magnetic field. Increasing the number of poles decreases synchronous speed at a given electrical frequency.

At 60 Hz:

  • 2 poles → 3600 RPM
  • 4 poles → 1800 RPM
  • 6 poles → 1200 RPM
  • 8 poles → 900 RPM

This is why the correct motor pole count is important when calculating speed from frequency.

What Is Motor Slip?

Synchronous speed is the speed of the rotating magnetic field. An induction motor's rotor normally runs slightly slower than that field.

The difference is called slip. Slip is necessary for an induction motor to produce torque.

Slip % = ((Synchronous RPM − Actual RPM) ÷ Synchronous RPM) × 100

For example, suppose a 4-pole motor operates at 60 Hz and its measured shaft speed is 1750 RPM.

Synchronous RPM = 1800
Actual RPM = 1750

Slip = ((1800 − 1750) ÷ 1800) × 100
Slip ≈ 2.78%

The amount of slip varies with motor design and operating load.

Frequency and VFDs

A variable-frequency drive changes the frequency supplied to an AC motor to control motor speed.

Because synchronous speed is proportional to frequency, reducing frequency generally reduces motor speed.

For example, a 4-pole motor operating at 40 Hz has a theoretical synchronous speed of:

RPM = (120 × 40) ÷ 4
RPM = 1200 RPM

The actual shaft speed will normally be somewhat lower for an induction motor because of slip.

Field Applications

Frequency-to-RPM calculations can be useful when troubleshooting:

  • VFD-driven motors
  • Pumps and fans
  • Conveyors
  • Compressors
  • Tachometer feedback
  • Encoder and speed-feedback systems
  • Process equipment
  • Motor nameplate information

For example, if a VFD is actually outputting 45 Hz to a 4-pole motor:

RPM = (120 × 45) ÷ 4
RPM = 1350 RPM

This is the theoretical synchronous speed. Actual motor shaft speed will depend on motor slip and load.

Motor Speed Troubleshooting

If calculated and measured motor speeds do not agree, check the entire signal and mechanical chain rather than immediately assuming the calculation is incorrect.

  1. Verify the motor pole count. Check the motor documentation or nameplate.
  2. Verify VFD output frequency. Confirm the actual output frequency rather than relying only on the speed command.
  3. Measure actual shaft speed. A tachometer or appropriate speed-feedback device can provide a direct measurement.
  4. Consider motor slip. An induction motor normally operates below synchronous speed.
  5. Check the load. Motor slip generally increases as load increases.
  6. Check speed feedback. If an encoder or tachometer is being used, verify scaling, pulses-per-revolution, wiring, and signal integrity.

Common Frequency and RPM Mistakes

  1. Treating synchronous RPM as actual shaft RPM. An induction motor normally runs below synchronous speed.
  2. Using the wrong motor pole count. Pole count has a direct effect on calculated speed.
  3. Assuming every 60 Hz motor runs at exactly the calculated RPM. Actual speed depends on motor characteristics and load.
  4. Confusing electrical frequency with mechanical RPM.
  5. Ignoring slip when comparing calculated and measured motor speed.
  6. Using a VFD speed command instead of actual output frequency.
  7. Ignoring gearboxes or other mechanical transmission ratios.

Gearboxes and Mechanical Speed Ratios

The frequency-to-RPM calculation gives the motor's theoretical synchronous speed. It does not automatically account for a gearbox, belt drive, pulley system, or other mechanical transmission.

For example, if a gearbox reduces motor speed by a ratio of 10:1, a motor turning at 1800 RPM would theoretically produce:

Output RPM = 1800 ÷ 10
Output RPM = 180 RPM

Actual output speed will depend on the motor's actual speed and the mechanical system.

Practical Field Workflow

When troubleshooting a motor-speed problem, work through the system in stages:

VFD frequency → theoretical motor speed → actual motor speed → mechanical transmission → final equipment speed

This separates electrical, motor, feedback, and mechanical problems instead of treating the entire system as one calculation.
