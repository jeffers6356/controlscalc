How Frequency and RPM Are Related

The rotational speed of an AC motor is closely related to the frequency of the electrical power supplied to it. Motor speed also depends on the number of magnetic poles in the motor.

This relationship is especially useful when working with motors, variable-frequency drives (VFDs), tachometers, speed feedback, and process equipment driven by AC motors.

Frequency → RPM Formula

The theoretical or synchronous speed of an AC motor is:

Synchronous RPM = (120 × Frequency) ÷ Number of Poles

The factor 120 comes from converting electrical cycles per second into revolutions per minute while accounting for the motor's magnetic poles.

RPM → Frequency Formula

If you know the desired synchronous speed and motor pole count, you can solve the formula for frequency:

Frequency = (RPM × Number of Poles) ÷ 120

Worked Example: 60 Hz, 4-Pole Motor

Given:
  • Frequency = 60 Hz
  • Motor = 4 poles

Synchronous RPM:

RPM = (120 × 60) ÷ 4
RPM = 1800
Result: The synchronous speed is 1800 RPM.

An actual 4-pole induction motor may run somewhat below 1800 RPM under load. For example, a motor nameplate might show a rated speed around 1750 RPM at 60 Hz.

Why Motor Poles Matter

Motor poles determine how many magnetic fields are established around the stator. Increasing the number of poles reduces the motor's synchronous speed at a given electrical frequency.

At 60 Hz:

  • 2 poles → 3600 RPM
  • 4 poles → 1800 RPM
  • 6 poles → 1200 RPM
  • 8 poles → 900 RPM

This is why selecting the correct motor pole count is essential when calculating speed from frequency.

What Is Motor Slip?

Synchronous speed is the theoretical speed of the rotating magnetic field. An induction motor's rotor normally runs slightly slower than that field.

The difference is called slip. Slip is necessary for an induction motor to produce torque.

Slip % = ((Synchronous RPM − Actual RPM) ÷ Synchronous RPM) × 100
Example:

A 4-pole motor operates at 60 Hz and has a measured shaft speed of 1750 RPM.

Synchronous RPM = 1800
Actual RPM = 1750

Slip = ((1800 − 1750) ÷ 1800) × 100
Slip ≈ 2.78%

Frequency and VFDs

A variable-frequency drive changes the frequency supplied to an AC motor to control motor speed.

Because synchronous speed is proportional to frequency, reducing frequency generally reduces motor speed.

Example:

A 4-pole motor is operated by a VFD at 40 Hz.

RPM = (120 × 40) ÷ 4
RPM = 1200 RPM

The theoretical synchronous speed is therefore 1200 RPM. Actual shaft speed will depend on motor load and slip.

Field Applications

This calculation can be useful when troubleshooting:

  • VFD-driven motors
  • Pumps and fans
  • Conveyors
  • Compressors
  • Tachometer feedback
  • Encoder and speed-feedback systems
  • Process equipment
  • Motor nameplate information

For example, if a VFD display shows 45 Hz and the motor is a 4-pole motor:

RPM = (120 × 45) ÷ 4
RPM = 1350 RPM

If the measured shaft speed is significantly different from the expected value, investigate the actual motor characteristics, load, VFD configuration, and feedback system.

Troubleshooting

If calculated and measured motor speeds do not agree, check the signal chain rather than immediately assuming the calculation is wrong.

  1. Verify the motor pole count. Check the motor documentation or nameplate.
  2. Verify VFD output frequency. Confirm the actual commanded/output frequency rather than assuming it from the speed command.
  3. Check actual shaft speed. A tachometer or appropriate speed-feedback device can provide a direct measurement.
  4. Consider motor slip. An induction motor normally operates below synchronous speed.
  5. Check the load. Increasing motor load can increase slip.
  6. Check speed feedback. If an encoder or tachometer is being used, verify scaling, pulses-per-revolution, wiring, and signal integrity.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating synchronous RPM as actual shaft RPM.
  • Using the wrong motor pole count.
  • Assuming every motor marked "60 Hz" runs at exactly the calculated synchronous speed.
  • Confusing electrical frequency with mechanical RPM.
  • Ignoring slip when comparing calculated speed with measured motor speed.
  • Using a speed command instead of the actual VFD output frequency.
  • Forgetting that some applications use gearboxes, belts, pulleys, or other mechanical speed ratios.

Gearboxes and Mechanical Speed Ratios

The frequency-to-RPM calculation gives the motor's theoretical synchronous speed. It does not automatically account for a gearbox or other mechanical transmission.

If a gearbox reduces motor speed by a ratio of 10:1, a motor turning at 1800 RPM would theoretically produce:

Output RPM = 1800 ÷ 10
Output RPM = 180 RPM

Actual output speed will depend on the motor's actual speed and the mechanical system.

Practical Field Workflow

When troubleshooting a motor-speed problem, use the following sequence:

VFD frequency → theoretical motor speed → actual motor speed → mechanical transmission → final equipment speed

This separates electrical, motor, feedback, and mechanical problems instead of treating the entire system as one calculation.
