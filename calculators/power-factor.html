Calculate Power Factor

What Is Power Factor?

Power factor describes how effectively an AC electrical system converts apparent power into real power.

PF = Real Power ÷ Apparent Power

Real power is the power actually doing useful work, such as turning a motor shaft, heating an element, or operating equipment.

Apparent power represents the combination of real and reactive power that the electrical system must supply.

Power factor is expressed as a number between 0 and 1, or sometimes as a percentage.

Important: Power factor is not the same thing as motor efficiency. Efficiency describes the conversion of electrical input power into mechanical output power. Power factor describes the relationship between real and apparent electrical power.

Real Power, Reactive Power, and Apparent Power

AC power systems are commonly described using three related quantities.

Real Power — kW

Real power is the portion of electrical power that performs useful work.

Real Power = kW

Apparent Power — kVA

Apparent power represents the total electrical loading seen by the source and conductors.

kVA = √(kW² + kVAR²)

Reactive Power — kVAR

Reactive power is associated with energy being alternately stored and returned by inductive and capacitive elements.

kVAR = √(kVA² − kW²)

These quantities form the familiar power triangle.

kVA² = kW² + kVAR²

3-Phase Power Factor Formula

For a balanced 3-phase system, apparent power can be calculated from line-to-line voltage and line current:

kVA = (√3 × V × I) ÷ 1000

Therefore:

PF = kW ÷ kVA

Combining the two equations gives:

PF = (kW × 1000) ÷ (√3 × V × I)
Voltage convention: For this 3-phase calculation, use line-to-line voltage. For example, a typical 480 V 3-phase system should be entered as 480 V, not 277 V.

Single-Phase Power Factor Formula

For a single-phase system:

kVA = (V × I) ÷ 1000

Therefore:

PF = (kW × 1000) ÷ (V × I)

Worked Example: 480 V, 3-Phase Load

Suppose a 480 V, 3-phase load is drawing 20 A and consuming 12 kW.

First calculate apparent power:

kVA = (√3 × 480 × 20) ÷ 1000
kVA ≈ 16.63

Then calculate power factor:

PF = 12 ÷ 16.63
PF ≈ 0.722
Result: PF ≈ 0.72

This means approximately 72% of the apparent power is being converted into real power for this simplified measurement.

Why Power Factor Affects Current

For a given amount of real power, a lower power factor requires more current.

Rearranging the 3-phase power equation:

I = (kW × 1000) ÷ (√3 × V × PF)

Because power factor appears in the denominator, reducing PF increases the required current for the same real power.

Example:

Consider a 12 kW load at 480 V.

At PF = 0.95, the current is approximately 15.20 A.

At PF = 0.70, the current increases to approximately 20.62 A.

The real power is the same in both cases, but the electrical system must carry more current when the power factor is lower.

Leading and Lagging Power Factor

Power factor can also be described by whether current leads or lags voltage.

Lagging Power Factor

Inductive loads such as motors and transformers commonly have lagging power factor.

Leading Power Factor

Capacitive loads can produce leading power factor.

The basic magnitude calculation performed by this calculator does not determine leading versus lagging. That requires information about the phase relationship or reactive power direction.

Power Factor and Motors

Motors are an important source of reactive power demand in industrial facilities.

Motor power factor changes with operating load. A motor operating lightly loaded can have a substantially different power factor than the same motor operating near its rated load.

This is one reason why a theoretical power-factor value should not automatically be assumed to be constant under all operating conditions.

When available, motor nameplate information and measured electrical data provide better information about the actual operating condition.

Power Factor vs. Efficiency

These two concepts are frequently confused.

Quantity What It Describes
Power Factor Relationship between real power and apparent power
Efficiency Relationship between useful output power and input power
kW Real electrical power
kVA Apparent electrical power
kVAR Reactive power
A motor can have both a power factor and an efficiency value. They describe different parts of the energy conversion process and should not be substituted for one another.

Field Troubleshooting

Measured current seems unusually high

Check power factor along with voltage and real power. A low power factor can cause higher current for the same real power.

Power factor changes with load

This can be normal for motors and other inductive equipment. Compare measurements at different operating conditions before assuming the equipment is malfunctioning.

Calculated PF is greater than 1

A power factor magnitude greater than 1 is not physically valid. Check:

  • Voltage measurement
  • Current measurement
  • Power measurement
  • Single-phase vs. 3-phase selection
  • Line-to-line vs. line-to-neutral voltage
  • Instrument configuration

Calculated PF does not match an electrical meter

Verify that the measurements represent the same operating condition and that the meter and calculation are using the same phase configuration and voltage convention.

Also remember that this basic calculation assumes a balanced system. Real installations can have voltage imbalance, harmonic distortion, and other effects that make simple calculations less representative.

Common Mistakes

  • Using line-to-neutral voltage in the 3-phase formula.
  • Forgetting the √3 factor for a 3-phase system.
  • Using a single-phase equation for a 3-phase load.
  • Confusing kW with kVA.
  • Confusing power factor with efficiency.
  • Assuming motor power factor is constant at every load.
  • Treating the calculated value as a substitute for actual measured power-factor data when accurate measurements are available.
