3-Phase Motor Current Calculator

Estimate the full-load current of a 3-phase motor from horsepower or kW, line voltage, power factor, and motor efficiency.

Calculate Motor Current

How 3-Phase Motor Current Is Calculated

A balanced 3-phase motor draws power from three AC phases. For a 3-phase system, electrical input power can be related to line current by:

P = √3 × V × I × PF

For a real motor, the electrical input power must be greater than the mechanical power delivered by the motor because motors are not 100% efficient.

When the motor power rating is given as mechanical output power, efficiency must therefore be included:

I = Pmechanical ÷ (√3 × V × PF × η)

where η is motor efficiency expressed as a decimal.

Horsepower Formula

One mechanical horsepower is approximately:

1 HP = 746 W

Therefore, for a motor rated in horsepower:

I = (HP × 746) ÷ (√3 × V × PF × η)

Worked Example: 10 HP, 480 V Motor

Assume:

  • Motor power = 10 HP
  • Voltage = 480 V
  • Power factor = 0.85
  • Efficiency = 90%

Convert horsepower to mechanical power:

10 × 746 = 7,460 W

Then calculate current:

I = 7,460 ÷ (√3 × 480 × 0.85 × 0.90)
I ≈ 11.76 A
Estimated current: approximately 11.8 A.

The actual nameplate full-load current may differ because the actual motor efficiency and power factor vary with motor design and operating conditions.

kW Formula

If motor power is already provided in kilowatts, convert kW to watts:

P(W) = kW × 1,000

The current equation becomes:

I = (kW × 1,000) ÷ (√3 × V × PF × η)

Example: 7.5 kW, 400 V Motor

Assume:

  • Motor power = 7.5 kW
  • Voltage = 400 V
  • Power factor = 0.82
  • Efficiency = 90%
I = 7,500 ÷ (√3 × 400 × 0.82 × 0.90)
I ≈ 14.70 A

Why √3 Appears in the Formula

The √3 term comes from the geometry and phase relationship of a balanced 3-phase electrical system.

When using the standard 3-phase power equation:

P = √3 × VL-L × I × PF

VL-L is the line-to-line voltage and I is the line current.

Important: This calculator expects the motor's line-to-line supply voltage, such as 480 V in a typical U.S. industrial system. Do not substitute line-to-neutral voltage into this equation.

Power Factor

Power factor describes the relationship between real power and apparent power in an AC electrical system.

PF = Real Power ÷ Apparent Power

A lower power factor means that more current is required to deliver the same amount of real power.

For example, holding all other values constant, a motor with a power factor of 0.80 will draw more current than one with a power factor of 0.95.

Actual motor power factor changes with load, so the value used for an estimate should be appropriate for the operating condition being considered.

Motor Efficiency

Efficiency accounts for the fact that a motor consumes more electrical power than the mechanical power it delivers.

Efficiency = Mechanical Output Power ÷ Electrical Input Power

For example, a motor operating at 90% efficiency requires approximately 1.111 times as much electrical input power as its mechanical output power.

Electrical Input = Mechanical Output ÷ 0.90

Efficiency should be entered as a decimal in the mathematical formula, even though the calculator accepts it as a percentage.

Calculated Current vs. Nameplate FLA

This calculator produces an estimated current based on the motor's power rating, voltage, power factor, and efficiency.

The motor nameplate may provide a specific full-load current rating based on the manufacturer's motor design and applicable standards.

Use the nameplate when it is available. A calculated value should not automatically replace the manufacturer's rated full-load current when selecting, configuring, or protecting equipment.

In the field, calculated current is particularly useful for understanding whether a measured value is in the expected general range and for estimating current when motor details are incomplete.

Motor Current and Operating Load

A motor's current is not necessarily constant throughout its operating range.

At reduced mechanical load, a motor may draw substantially less current than its full-load current.

This is especially important when troubleshooting a motor that appears to be operating normally but is drawing less current than expected.

Conversely, sustained current above the expected operating range can indicate excessive mechanical load, voltage problems, phase imbalance, motor problems, or other issues.

Field Troubleshooting

Motor current is higher than expected

Check:

  • Mechanical load
  • Supply voltage
  • Phase-to-phase voltage balance
  • Motor nameplate rating
  • Motor connection
  • Motor condition
  • Bearing or driven-equipment problems
  • Power quality

Motor current is lower than expected

A low current reading is not automatically a problem. Determine the actual mechanical load first.

Also verify that the motor is operating at the expected voltage and that the measurement is being made correctly.

One phase has substantially different current

Compare all three phase currents and investigate:

  • Voltage imbalance
  • Loose or damaged connections
  • Contactor problems
  • Motor winding issues
  • Supply problems
  • Uneven phase loading
Don't diagnose from current alone. Motor current should be considered together with voltage, mechanical load, motor temperature, nameplate data, and the operating condition.

Common Mistakes

  • Using line-to-neutral voltage instead of line-to-line voltage.
  • Forgetting to include power factor.
  • Forgetting motor efficiency when converting mechanical output power to electrical input power.
  • Entering efficiency as 90 instead of 0.90 when doing the calculation manually.
  • Assuming calculated current must exactly match nameplate FLA.
  • Assuming motor current is constant regardless of mechanical load.
  • Confusing HP with electrical kW.
  • Using single-phase formulas for a 3-phase motor.
