How Flow Transmitter Scaling Works

A flow measurement can be represented by a 4–20 mA signal just like pressure, level, or temperature. The important difference is that some flow measurement technologies produce a nonlinear relationship between the measured signal and the actual flow.

Differential-pressure flow measurement is the most common example. Devices such as an orifice plate, venturi, or flow nozzle create a pressure difference that is related to flow approximately by:

Q ∝ √DP

In other words, flow is proportional to the square root of differential pressure.

This is why square-root extraction may be required somewhere in the measurement system.

Linear Scaling vs. Square-Root Extraction

If a transmitter's 4–20 mA output is already linear with flow, normal linear scaling is appropriate.

If the 4–20 mA signal represents differential pressure and the square root has not already been extracted, the PLC or another device must perform square-root extraction before interpreting the signal as flow.

Signal Linear Signal % Square-Root Flow %
4 mA 0% 0%
8 mA 25% 50%
12 mA 50% 70.7%
16 mA 75% 86.6%
20 mA 100% 100%
Important: Square-root extraction should only be applied when the signal represents a quantity that has a square-root relationship to the desired measurement. Do not automatically apply SRE to every flow transmitter.

The Linear Scaling Formula

For a linear 4–20 mA flow signal:

Flow = LRV + ((mA − 4) ÷ 16) × (URV − LRV)

For example, suppose a transmitter is configured for 0–100 GPM and the measured signal is 12 mA.

Flow = 0 + ((12 − 4) ÷ 16) × 100

Flow = 50 GPM

This is correct when the transmitter output is linear with flow.

The Square-Root Formula

When the 4–20 mA signal represents differential pressure and square-root extraction is required, first determine the percentage of signal span:

Signal % = (mA − 4) ÷ 16

Then take the square root:

Flow % = √(Signal %)

The complete calculation is:

Flow = LRV + √((mA − 4) ÷ 16) × (URV − LRV)

Example: 0–100 GPM at 12 mA

First calculate the signal percentage:

(12 − 4) ÷ 16 = 0.50

Then take the square root:

√0.50 = 0.7071

Therefore:

Flow = 0.7071 × 100

Flow ≈ 70.7 GPM

Notice the important difference: 12 mA corresponds to 50% of signal span, but approximately 70.7% of flow span when square-root extraction is required.

Where Should Square-Root Extraction Occur?

Square-root extraction can occur in different parts of a control system.

1. Inside the transmitter

Some differential-pressure transmitters can be configured to perform square-root extraction internally. In that case, the transmitter's 4–20 mA output may already be linear with flow.

2. Inside the PLC

The transmitter may instead send a signal proportional to differential pressure, while the PLC performs the square-root calculation.

3. Inside another flow computer or control system

A dedicated flow computer, DCS, or other control device may perform the extraction and additional flow calculations.

Watch for double square-root extraction. If the transmitter has already linearized the signal and the PLC applies another square root, the resulting flow value will be incorrect.

Field Application

A common application is measuring flow through an orifice plate. The orifice creates a differential pressure across the plate. As flow increases, differential pressure increases according to a nonlinear relationship.

The general signal path may look like this:

Process Flow → Differential Pressure → DP Transmitter → 4–20 mA → Analog Input → Scaling → Square Root → Flow → HMI / SCADA

However, the actual sequence depends on how the transmitter and control system have been configured.

When troubleshooting a flow measurement, determine exactly where the square root is being performed before changing the PLC logic.

Troubleshooting Flow Scaling

Flow is approximately correct at high flow but wrong at low flow

Check whether square-root extraction is required and whether it is being performed in the correct location.

Flow appears much too high at moderate signal levels

Check for an incorrect scaling formula, incorrect transmitter range, or square-root extraction being applied when it should not be.

Flow appears unusually low

Check whether the square root is missing from a DP-based measurement.

The value changed dramatically after a transmitter replacement

Compare the configuration of the old and new transmitters. Important parameters can include:

  • LRV and URV
  • Pressure range
  • Output mode
  • Square-root configuration
  • Damping
  • Engineering units
  • Failure/current alarm settings

The HMI value does not match a local flow indication

Trace the signal from the field device through the entire control system:

Process → Transmitter → mA Signal → Analog Input → Raw Counts → Scaling → SRE → Engineering Units → HMI

Common Mistakes

  • Applying square-root extraction to a transmitter that is already linearized.
  • Forgetting square-root extraction on a DP flow signal.
  • Applying square root twice.
  • Using the wrong transmitter LRV or URV.
  • Assuming all flow transmitters use differential pressure.
  • Confusing percentage of signal span with percentage of flow.
  • Changing PLC scaling without checking the transmitter configuration first.
  • Ignoring the analog input module's actual raw-count range.

Practical Field Workflow

  1. Identify the flow measurement technology.
  2. Determine whether the transmitter measures flow directly or measures differential pressure.
  3. Check the transmitter's configured LRV and URV.
  4. Determine whether the transmitter performs square-root extraction.
  5. Measure the actual 4–20 mA signal.
  6. Verify the analog input's raw value.
  7. Verify PLC scaling.
  8. Verify where square-root extraction occurs.
  9. Compare the calculated flow with the expected process condition.
Good troubleshooting rule: Don't start by changing the PLC calculation. First establish what the field device is actually sending and what the transmitter is configured to do.
