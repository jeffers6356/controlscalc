Understanding the 0–10 V Signal

A 0–10 V signal is an analog voltage signal commonly used to command or represent a continuously variable value.

In building automation, for example, a controller may send 0–10 V to a valve actuator, damper actuator, or variable-speed drive. A sensor or transmitter may also use a voltage signal to represent a measured process value.

The exact meaning of the voltage depends on the equipment configuration. A common arrangement is:

0 V = 0%
5 V = 50%
10 V = 100%

However, not every application uses the full 0–10 V range. Always verify the intended range in the equipment documentation.

0–10 V Quick Reference

Signal Approximate Output
0 V 0%
1 V 10%
2 V 20%
5 V 50%
7.5 V 75%
10 V 100%

This assumes a linear 0–10 V signal where 0 V represents the bottom of the range and 10 V represents the top.

How to Measure a 0–10 V Signal

A digital multimeter set to DC voltage is normally sufficient to check a 0–10 V control signal.

  1. Set the meter to DC voltage.
  2. Identify the signal terminal.
  3. Identify the signal reference or common terminal.
  4. Place the meter leads across the signal and reference.
  5. Compare the measured voltage with the expected signal.
Important: Do not assume that every terminal labeled COM is connected to the same reference. Verify the wiring and equipment documentation before making measurements or connections.

Measuring from the signal terminal to the wrong reference can produce a misleading reading even when the signal source is functioning correctly.

Check the Signal Source

If a 0–10 V signal is incorrect, begin by determining whether the problem is at the source or somewhere downstream.

Examples of signal sources include:

  • PLC analog outputs
  • DDC controller outputs
  • HVAC controllers
  • Signal converters
  • Transducers
  • Building automation controllers

Check what the controller is actually commanding. If the software says the output should be 7 V but the physical output measures 0 V, the problem is likely upstream of the receiving device.

Example:

Controller output command = 70%
Expected voltage = approximately 7 V
Measured voltage = 0.2 V

This suggests the problem should be investigated at the controller output, wiring, reference, or output configuration rather than assuming the actuator itself is faulty.

Check the Receiving Device

Once the source has been verified, measure the signal at the receiving device.

Common receiving devices include:

  • Valve actuators
  • Damper actuators
  • VFD analog inputs
  • PLC analog inputs
  • DDC analog inputs
  • Signal conditioners

Compare the voltage at the source with the voltage at the receiving device.

Useful diagnostic: If the voltage is correct at the source but incorrect at the receiving device, focus on the wiring, common/reference, connections, and loading between those points.

Check the Wiring

0–10 V systems are generally sensitive to wiring and reference problems because the receiving device measures voltage relative to a reference.

Common Wiring Problems

  • Open signal conductor
  • Open common/reference conductor
  • Loose terminal
  • Incorrect terminal connection
  • Shorted signal conductor
  • Shared or incorrect reference
  • Damaged cable
  • Incorrect polarity or terminal assignment

If possible, compare the voltage directly at the source and directly at the load. This can quickly identify whether the signal is being lost between the two points.

What Different Readings Can Mean

Measured Signal Possible Investigation
0 V Check source output, power, wiring, reference, configuration, and output command.
Approximately expected voltage Signal source and measurement reference may be functioning correctly. Investigate the receiving device if the process is still incorrect.
Higher than expected Check output configuration, scaling, reference, and whether another signal is influencing the measurement.
Unstable or rapidly changing Check controller behavior, process conditions, wiring, reference stability, noise, and grounding.
Correct at source, wrong at load Investigate wiring, terminals, reference, and loading.

These readings are diagnostic starting points rather than definitive fault identifications. The equipment's wiring and specifications should always be checked.

Check Signal Scaling

A physically correct voltage can still produce an incorrect system response if the signal scaling is wrong.

For a simple 0–10 V signal representing 0–100%:

Percentage = Voltage ÷ 10 × 100
A controller output of:

6 V

corresponds to:

6 ÷ 10 × 100 = 60%

However, a device may use a different engineering range. For example, 0–10 V might represent 0–100 PSI, −40–250 °F, or another configured range.

Check both the electrical signal range and the engineering range.

Ground and Reference Problems

Voltage signals depend on the reference used by the measuring device. Problems can occur when the signal source and receiving device do not share the expected reference.

Symptoms can include:

  • Unexpected voltage readings
  • Signal offset
  • Unstable readings
  • Signal that changes when other equipment operates
  • Different readings when measured at different points

Verify the manufacturer's wiring requirements before bonding commons or grounds together. Do not assume that connecting two references together is always appropriate.

Field Troubleshooting Workflow

  1. Determine the expected signal. Find the controller command, configuration, or device documentation.
  2. Measure at the source. Determine whether the controller is actually producing the expected voltage.
  3. Measure at the receiving device. Compare the reading with the source.
  4. Verify the reference. Make sure the meter and receiving device are using the correct signal reference.
  5. Inspect wiring. Check terminals, conductors, polarity, and connections.
  6. Verify configuration. Check input/output type, signal range, scaling, and operating mode.
  7. Check the receiving device. If the correct voltage reaches the device but the output or process is incorrect, investigate the actuator, transducer, or input configuration.
Good troubleshooting practice: Make one measurement at a time and use each measurement to narrow the fault location. Avoid changing multiple settings before establishing what the signal is actually doing.

Common Fault Examples

Example 1: Controller Says 50%, Actuator Does Not Move

A controller commands 50% output. A measurement at the controller shows approximately 5 V, but the actuator terminal measures 0 V.

The next investigation should focus on the wiring, common, terminals, and connections between the controller and actuator.

Example 2: Actuator Receives 5 V but Moves to the Wrong Position

If the actuator is receiving the expected voltage, investigate the actuator configuration and scaling. The actuator may be configured for a different input range or operating mode.

Example 3: 10 V Command Produces Only Half Output

Verify the receiving device's input configuration. It may be configured for 0–20 V, 0–5 V, or another range rather than 0–10 V.

Example 4: Signal Changes When Another Device Starts

Investigate reference connections, grounding, shared wiring, power supplies, and electrical noise. Measure the signal and reference directly while the condition occurs.
